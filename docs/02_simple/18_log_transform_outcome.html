44  Log-Transformed Outcomes

44.1 Learning Objective

Interpret the coefficients when the outcome has been logged.

44.2 Lesson

44.2.1 Problem

When we log the outcome, the linearity assumption becomes \[ E\left(\log Y_i \middle| X_i\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i \text{.} \] This poses problems for interpretation; we’d like to talk about the untransformed outcome, but \[ E\left(\log Y_i \middle| X_i\right) \ne \log E\left(Y_i \middle| X_i\right) \text{.} \] So, for example, our interpretation of \(\beta_0\) is “the expected value of the log of the outcome when \(X_i = 0\).” That’s not very useful. Also, \[ \begin{align*} \beta_1 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1(x+1) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right) \\ &= E\left(\log Y_i \middle| X_i = x + 1\right) - E\left(\log Y_i \middle| X_i = x\right) \text{,} \end{align*} \] which isn’t very useful, either.

44.2.2 More Assumptions

Suppose we change our model to \[ \log Y_i|X_i \sim N\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i,\sigma^2\right) \text{.} \] Then the coefficients are more interpretable. Specifically, \[ \begin{align*} E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = 0\right) &= \exp\left(\beta_0 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) \\ \frac{E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x+1\right)}{E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x\right)} &= \exp\left(\beta_1\right) \text{.} \end{align*} \] In words, when the explanatory variable equals 0, we would expect the outcome variable to equal \[ \exp\left(\beta_0 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) \text{.} \] An increase of one unit in the explanatory variable is associated with a \[ \left(\exp\left(\beta_1\right) - 1\right) \times 100\% \] increase in the outcome variable. (If this is negative, it’s a decrease.) Another way of phrasing this is that a population with the explanatory variable equal to \(x + 1\) is expected to have an outcome \(\exp(\beta_1)\) times that of a population with the explanatory variable equal to \(x\).

Note that, in order to interpret the coefficients this way, all four assumptions need to be met in the data. Also, you need to have used the natural log (base \(e\)) in order to transform the outcome. If you use any other base, the interpretation above is not justified.

44.2.3 Math

I have a tendency to forget the math behind these interpretations, so I’m going to store it here. You don’t need to know it; it’s more for me.

CautionCaution: Math

Define \(Z_i = \log Y_i\). Then \[ Z_i|X_i \sim N\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i,\sigma^2\right) \] and \[ f(z|x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\left(z - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \text{.} \] Further, \[ Y_i = \exp\left(Z_i\right) \] and \[ \begin{align*} E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x\right) &= E\left(\exp\left(Z_i\right)\middle|X_i = x\right) \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left(z\right) f(z|x)\,dz \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left(z\right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\left(z - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(z-\frac{\left(z - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(z-\frac{z^2 - 2z\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right) + \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{z^2 - 2z\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right) - 2z\sigma^2 + \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{z^2 - 2z\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x+\sigma^2\right) + \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{z^2 - 2z\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x+\sigma^2\right) + \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sigma^2\right)^2 - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sigma^2\right)^2 + \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \exp\left(\frac{\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sigma^2\right)^2 - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{z^2 - 2z\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x+\sigma^2\right) + \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \sigma^2\right)^2 }{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \exp\left(\frac{2\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)\sigma^2 + \sigma^4}{2\sigma^2}\right) \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\left(z - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x - \sigma^2\right)^2 }{2\sigma^2}\right) \,dz \\ &= \exp\left(\frac{2\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right)\sigma^2 + \sigma^4}{2\sigma^2}\right) \\ &= \exp\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) \text{.} \\ \end{align*} \]

Thus, \[ E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = 0\right) = \exp\left(\beta_0 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) \] and \[ \begin{align*} \frac{E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x+1\right)}{E\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x\right)} &= \frac{\exp\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (x+1) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)}{\exp\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)} \\ &= \exp\left(\beta_1\right) \text{.} \end{align*} \]

44.2.4 Forest Fire Example

Let’s return to the forest fire example. Each observation is a forest fire in Montesinho Park (Portugal) between January 2000 and December 2003. In the scatterplot below, I’ve plotted total burned area (in hectares) as a function of relative humidity (%).

It’s hard to detect a relationship between the two variables. This might be easier if we log the outcome. However, we can’t take the log of zero, and 47.8% of the observations have zero as the outcome.

It appears that there is lower variability in the residuals for higher relative humidities, but that may be because there are just fewer observations at the higher relative humidities. Note that \[ \begin{align*} \hat{\beta}_0 &= 2.064810 \\ \hat{\beta}_1 &= -0.005030 \\ \hat{\sigma} &= 1.528 \text{,} \end{align*} \] so

\[ \begin{align*} \hat{E}\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = 0\right) &= \exp\left(\hat{\beta}_0 + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) \\ &= \exp\left(2.064810 + \frac{1.528^2}{2}\right) \\ &= 25.3 \\ \frac{\hat{E}\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x+1\right)}{\hat{E}\left(Y_i\middle|X_i = x\right)} &= \exp\left(\hat{\beta}_1\right) \\ &= \exp\left(-0.005030 \right) \\ &= 0.995 \text{.} \end{align*} \] This means that, when the relative humidity is 0%, we would expect 25.3 hectares to burn. For each additional percentage point of relative humidity, we would expect a decrease of 0.5% in total area burned.

44.3 Problems

In the past, when I’ve forgotten how to interpret regression coefficients with a log-transformed outcome, I’ve found my way to this page from the UCLA Statistical Consulting Group: How do I interpret a regression model when some variables are log transformed?. They recommend using the geometric mean in your interpretations. I don’t think this is a good idea, since (almost) nobody understands the geometric mean.

There are six variables in this data set: reading, writing, and math scores; log math and writing scores; and gender (or maybe sex).

44.3.1 Problem 1

One of the first things they do is regress the log of the writing score on gender. Based on the following two boxplots, what is the impact of logging the writing score?

TipSolution

Logging the writing score makes the distribution of the outcome more skewed! Also, whether you log the outcome or not, the variability of the outcome is different for males and for females, so we’re violating the assumption of homoscedasticity.
