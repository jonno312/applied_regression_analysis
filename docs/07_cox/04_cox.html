87  Cox Proportional Hazards Model

87.1 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the assumptions of the Cox proportional hazards model.
  • Interpret the coefficients in a Cox proportional hazards model.
  • Explain what “proportional hazards” means.

87.2 Lesson

87.2.1 Assumptions

The Cox proportional hazards model has the following assumptions, where \(h_i(t)\) is the hazard rate for the \(i\)th observation and \(h_0(t)\) is the baseline hazard rate:

  1. \(\log h_i(t) = \log h_0(t) + \beta_1 X_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip}\)
  2. The observations are iid.
  3. Censoring is independent of time to event.

Note that there’s no \(\beta_0\). With the Cox proportional hazards model (which I will abbreviate to the Cox model), we don’t estimate an intercept. This means we don’t estimate the baseline hazard rate. At no point do we say what \(h_0(t)\) is.

87.2.2 Interpretation

How do we interpret the coefficients in the Cox model? Consider the first assumption: \[ \log h_i(t) = \log h_0(t) + \beta_1 X_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} \text{.} \] If we exponentiate both sides, we get this: \[ h_i(t) = h_0(t) \exp\left( \beta_1 X_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} \right) \text{.} \] So, we can get \(\exp(\beta_1)\) as follows: \[ \begin{align*} \frac{h_0(t) \exp\left( \beta_1 (x+1) + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} \right)}{h_0(t) \exp\left( \beta_1 x + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} \right)} &= \frac{\exp\left( \beta_1 (x+1) + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} \right)}{\exp\left( \beta_1 x + \cdots + \beta_p X_{ip} \right)} \\ &= \frac{\exp\left( \beta_1 (x+1) \right)}{\exp\left( \beta_1 x \right)} \\ &= \frac{\exp\left( \beta_1 x + \beta_1 \right)}{\exp\left( \beta_1 x \right)} \\ &= \exp\left(\beta_1\right) \text{.} \end{align*} \]

So, \(\exp\left(\beta_1\right)\) is the hazard ratio at time \(t\) comparing two populations that are identical except for a difference of one unit on the first explanatory variable. Phrased another way, the hazard rate at time \(t\) for a population with \(X_1 = x+1\) is \(\exp(\beta_1)\) times the hazard rate at time \(t\) for a population with \(X_1 = x\), holding all else equal. Phrased yet another way, the hazard rate at time \(t\) for a population with \(X_1 = x+1\) is \(\left(\exp(\beta_1) - 1\right)\times 100\%\) greater than the hazard rate at time \(t\) for a population with \(X_1 = x\), holding all else equal.

87.2.3 Example

Let’s look at data from a trial comparing two adjuvant therapy regimens for colon cancer (Moertel et al., 1995). These are the variables:

  • time: Time until death in days. Unfortunately, I don’t know the time origin.
  • status: 1 is death, 0 is censoring.
  • rx: This is the treatment variable, and it has three levels: Obs for observation; Lev for Levamisole, and Lev+5FU for Levamisole plus 5-FU.
  • sex: 1 for male, 0 for female.
  • age: This is in years, but I don’t know when it was measured. Maybe at the time origin?

The results of a Cox regression is below.

# Cox regression
mod_01 <- coxph(formula = Surv(time = time,
                               event = status) ~ rx + sex + age,
                data = d_colon)
summary(mod_01)

  • The 0.9729 next to rxLev means that the hazard rate at time \(t\) for participants on Levamisole is 13% lower than the hazard rate for participants on observation, holding sex and age constant.
  • The 0.6881 next to rxLev+5FU means that the hazard rate at time \(t\) for participants on Levamisole plus 5-FU is 31% lower than the hazard rate for participants on observation, holding sex and age constant.
  • The 0.9996 next to sex means that the hazard at time \(t\) for males is 0.04% lower than the hazard rate for females, holding treatment and age constant.
  • The 1.0024 next to age means that with each additional year of age, the hazard rate at time \(t\) increases by 0.24%, holding treatment and sex constant.

87.2.4 Proportional Hazards

Note that I kept saying “hazard rate at time \(t\)”. With the Cox proportional hazards model, we assume that if you fix \(t\), one hazard rate is a constant multiple of the other. In other words, the hazard rates are always proportional to each other. But we must fix the time we’re talking about. We can’t compare the hazard rate in one group at time \(t\) to the hazard rate in another group at time \(s\).

87.3 Problems

87.3.1 Problem 1

To quote the help page for this data set, “The 197 patients in this dataset were a 50% random sample of the patients with ‘high-risk’ diabetic retinopathy as defined by the Diabetic Retinopathy Study (DRS). Each patient had one eye randomized to laser treatment and the other eye received no treatment. For each eye, the event of interest was the time from initiation of treatment to the time when visual acuity dropped below 5/200 two visits in a row. Thus there is a built-in lag time of approximately 6 months (visits were every 3 months). Survival times in this dataset are therefore the actual time to blindness in months, minus the minimum possible time to event (6.5 months). Censoring was caused by death, dropout, or end of the study.”

Since I want the observations to be independent, I’m going to only use the left eye for each patient. The explanatory variables are as follows:

  • age: Age at diagnosis.
  • trt: Treatment (0 = no treatment, 1 = laser).
