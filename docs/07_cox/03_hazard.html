86  Hazard Rate

86.1 Learning Objectives

  • Define, use, and interpret discrete hazard.
  • Define, use, and interpret hazard rate.

86.2 Lesson

The hazard rate is a very important concept in survival analysis, but it’s also very difficult to define and interpret. So, we’re going to start with something easier, which I will call the discrete hazard or discrete time hazard.

86.2.1 Discrete Hazard

If \(T\) is the time to an event, and time is measured in discrete units, then the discrete hazard is \(P(T = t| T \ge t)\). In words, the discrete hazard at time \(t\) is the probability that the event occurs at time \(t\), given that it has not occurred before time \(t\).

What do I mean by “discrete time”? I mean that time is measured in units that don’t allow fractions. For example, imagine time is measured in days, where you can’t have any partial days. Or, imagine time is measured in the number of trials, like the number of times you turn on a light before the bulb dies.

Note that the discrete hazard is a probability, but it’s a conditional probability. In general, \(P(T = t| T \ge t) \ne P(T = t)\). To illustrate this, suppose \(T\) is time from birth to death, measured in whole years. \(P(T = 100)\) is probably pretty low; very few people survive to age 100. In contrast, \(P(T = 100|T \ge 100)\) is probably relatively high. Among all 100-year-olds, a relatively high percentage die before turning 101.

86.2.2 Hazard Rate

Now suppose time is measured as a continuous variable, so it can take any positive decimal value. For any value \(t\), \(P(T = t) = 0\). So, \(P(T = t| T \ge t) = 0\), which is not very useful to us. So, we have to use limits.

Consider the quantity \[ \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\delta} \text{.} \] When \(\delta = 1\), we essentially have our discrete time hazard: \[ \begin{align*} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\delta} &= \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + 1 \middle| T \ge t\right)}{1} \\ &= P\left(t \le T < t + 1 \middle| T \ge t\right) \text{.} \\ \end{align*} \]

To get a hazard rate, we’re going to make \(\delta\) smaller and smaller. \[ \begin{align*} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + 1 \middle| T \ge t\right)}{1} &= P\left(t \le T < t + 1 \middle| T \ge t\right) \\ \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{2} \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\frac{1}{2}} &= 2P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{2} \middle| T \ge t\right) \\ \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{3} \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\frac{1}{3}} &= 3P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{3} \middle| T \ge t\right) \\ \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{4} \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\frac{1}{4}} &= 4P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{4} \middle| T \ge t\right) \\ \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{5} \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\frac{1}{5}} &= 5P\left(t \le T < t + \frac{1}{5} \middle| T \ge t\right) \\ \end{align*} \]

As \(\delta\) gets smaller, \(P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \middle| T \ge t\right)\) gets smaller, too, but \(\frac{1}{\delta}\) gets bigger. So, by dividing by \(\delta\), we’re balancing out the decrease in \(P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \middle| T \ge t\right)\).

We define the hazard rate to be \[ h(t) = \lim_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\delta} \text{.} \] This is also \[ \begin{align*} h(t) &= \lim_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \middle| T \ge t\right)}{\delta} \\ &= \lim_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta , T \ge t\right)}{\delta P\left( T \ge t\right)} \\ &= \lim_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \right)}{\delta P\left( T \ge t\right)} \\ &= \frac{1}{P\left( T \ge t\right)} \lim_{\delta\downarrow 0} \frac{P\left(t \le T < t + \delta \right)}{\delta } \\ &= \frac{1}{P\left( T \ge t\right)} f(t) \\ &= \frac{f(t)}{P\left( T \ge t\right)} \\ &= \frac{f(t)}{P\left( T > t\right)} \text{.} \\ \end{align*} \] The numerator is the probability density function or PDF. The denominator is the survival function.

The hazard rate is, unfortunately, very difficult to interpret. It’s a conditional probability per unit time. It’s like a PDF in that we arrive at it via a limit, but it’s unlike a PDF in that we don’t get a probability if we integrate it. Some people interpret the hazard rate as “instantaneous risk”, which is pretty close to correct. The hazard rate is the risk (probability) that the event happens right now, given it hasn’t happened before now, scaled by time.

86.3 Problems

86.3.1 Problem 1

86.3.1.1 Part (a)

Is it possible for \(h(t) = 5\)? Why or why not?

TipSolution

Yes, this is possible. Since \(h(t)\) is not a probability, we can have \(h(t) > 1\). If \(f(t) = 5 \exp(-5t)\), then \(h(t) = 5\).

86.3.1.2 Part (b)

Is it possible for \(h(t) = -5\)? Why or why not?

TipSolution

No, we can’t have a negative hazard rate. In the definition, all the terms are non-negative, so we can’t end up with a negative value.

86.3.1.3 Part (c)

Is it possible for \(P(T = t|T \ge t) = 5\)? Why or why not?

TipSolution

No. This is a probability, so it must be between 0 and 1.

For generic \(w\), the discrete time hazard is \[ \begin{align*} h(w) &= P(W_i = w|W_i \ge w) \\ &= \frac{P(W_i = w,W_i \ge w)}{P(W_i \ge w)} \\ &= \frac{P(W_i = w)}{P(W_i \ge w)} \text{.} \end{align*} \] These are the hazards for specific values: \[ \begin{align*} h(2) &= \frac{P(W_i = 2)}{P(W_i \ge 2)} \\ &= \frac{0.00364}{1} \\ &= 0.00364 \\ h(3) &= \frac{P(W_i = 3)}{P(W_i \ge 3)} \\ &= \frac{0.00364}{1 - 0.00364} \\ &= \frac{\frac{1}{275}}{1-\frac{1}{275}} \\ &= \frac{1}{275-1} \\ &= 0.00365 \\ h(4) &= \frac{P(W_i = 4)}{P(W_i \ge 4)} \\ &= \frac{1}{1+1+1+10+2+258} \\ &= \frac{1}{273} \\ &= 0.00366 \\ h(5) &= \frac{P(W_i = 5)}{P(W_i \ge 5)} \\ &= 0 \\ h(6) &= \frac{P(W_i = 6)}{P(W_i \ge 6)} \\ &= \frac{1}{272} \\ &= 0.00368 \\ h(7) &= \frac{P(W_i = 7)}{P(W_i \ge 7)} \\ &= \frac{1}{271} \\ &= 0.00369 \\ h(8) &= \frac{P(W_i = 8)}{P(W_i \ge 8)} \\ &= \frac{10}{270} \\ &= 0.03704 \\ h(9) &= \frac{P(W_i = 9)}{P(W_i \ge 9)} \\ &= \frac{2}{260} \\ &= 0.00769 \\ h(10) &= \frac{P(W_i = 10)}{P(W_i \ge 10)} \\ &= \frac{258}{258} \\ &= 1 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

86.3.2.2 Part (b)

Compare and contrast the example above with this example:

Suppose you are studying a new face wash for treating acne. You give the face wash to \(n\) people and ask them to use it every day. Let \(X_i\) be the number of days until the \(i\)th person’s acne disappears. (This count includes the last day, when the acne is gone.) You decide that \(X_1,\dotsc,X_n\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{geometric}(p)\), where \(p\) is the probability that the acne disappears on a given day.

A random variable follows a geometric distribution if it represents the number of trials until the first success; there are only two outcomes (success and failure) on each trial; the trials are independent; and the probability of success is the same for each trial. (The parameter \(p\) is the probability of success on each trial.)

TipSolution

The Hot Ones example is sort of the opposite of the face wash example. In Hot Ones, \(P(W_i = w)\) is constant for five of the possible values of \(w\). The hazard, in contrast, is increasing. In the face wash example, \(P(X_i = x)\) is decreasing and the discrete time hazard is constant.

86.3.2.3 Part (c)

Which is greater, \(P(W_i = w)\) or \(h(w)\)? Is this true in general?

TipSolution

In this example and in general, \(h(w) \ge P(W_i = w)\). This is because \[ h(w) = \frac{P(W_i = w)}{P(W_i \ge w)} \] and \(P(W_i \ge w) \le 1\). Dividing by something smaller than 1 results in something bigger than what you started with.
