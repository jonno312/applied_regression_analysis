57  Correlated Covariates

57.1 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the effects of correlated explanatory variables.
  • Define, use, and interpret variance inflation factors (VIFs).

57.2 Lesson

It is not uncommon for the explanatory variables in a regression model to be correlated with each other. Usually, you’ve chosen the explanatory variables because you think they are related to the outcome, and if they’re all related to the outcome, they’re probably related to each other. Unfortunately, problems can arise when explanatory variables are highly correlated with each other, which we will examine here.

First, let’s consider the case where two explanatory variables are perfectly correlated, i.e., one is a linear function of the other. Suppose our outcome is body fat percentage and the explanatory variables are weight in pounds and weight in ounces. Weight in ounces is just weight in pounds times 16.


Call:
lm(formula = Bodyfat ~ Weight_pounds + Weight_ounces, data = d)

Residuals:

-17.0346  -3.9516  -0.3173   4.1811  19.1868

               Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)    
(Intercept)   -10.09418    3.95883  -2.550   0.0123 *  
Weight_pounds   0.16171    0.02201   7.347  6.1e-11 ***
Weight_ounces        NA         NA      NA       NA    
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Residual standard error: 6.462 on 98 degrees of freedom
Multiple R-squared:  0.3552,    Adjusted R-squared:  0.3486 
F-statistic: 53.98 on 1 and 98 DF,  p-value: 6.1e-11

As you can see, R did not even estimate anything for weight in ounces. If you try to include an explanatory variable that is a linear combination of any of the other explanatory variables, R will refuse. This is because it’s impossible to do the math required to get estimates, standard errors, etc.

What if you have two explanatory variables that are not perfectly correlated, but are still highly correlated? Let’s regress body fat percentage on weight in pounds and abdomen circumference in centimeters. These two explanatory variables have a correlation of 0.888.

cor(d$Weight_pounds,
    d$Abdomen)
[1] 0.8883167
mod_02 <- lm(formula = Bodyfat ~ Weight_pounds + Abdomen,
             data = d)
summary(mod_02)

Call:
lm(formula = Bodyfat ~ Weight_pounds + Abdomen, data = d)

Residuals:
    Min      1Q  Median      3Q     Max 
-9.5953 -2.9776 -0.0177  2.8974  9.1920 

Coefficients:
               Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)    
(Intercept)   -48.77854    4.18098 -11.667  < 2e-16 ***
Weight_pounds  -0.16082    0.03101  -5.185 1.18e-06 ***
Abdomen         1.04408    0.08918  11.707  < 2e-16 ***
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Residual standard error: 4.181 on 97 degrees of freedom
Multiple R-squared:  0.7328,    Adjusted R-squared:  0.7273 
F-statistic:   133 on 2 and 97 DF,  p-value: < 2.2e-16

That worked. Does that mean there’s no problem? Maybe. Watch what we get if we just use weight in pounds.


Call:
lm(formula = Bodyfat ~ Weight_pounds, data = d)

Residuals:
     Min       1Q   Median       3Q      Max 
-17.0346  -3.9516  -0.3173   4.1811  19.1868 

Coefficients:
               Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)    
(Intercept)   -10.09418    3.95883  -2.550   0.0123 *  
Weight_pounds   0.16171    0.02201   7.347  6.1e-11 ***
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Residual standard error: 6.462 on 98 degrees of freedom
Multiple R-squared:  0.3552,    Adjusted R-squared:  0.3486 
F-statistic: 53.98 on 1 and 98 DF,  p-value: 6.1e-11

What changed? The estimated coefficient on Weight_pounds flipped signs. When both variables are included in the model, the coefficient on Weight_pounds is -0.16082, meaning we would expect body fat percentage to decrease by 0.16 percentage points with every additional pound of body weight, holding abdomen circumference constant. If someone’s abdomen circumference is staying the same but they are getting heavier, they are probably getting taller or more muscular. Either way, their body fat is decreasing.

When only Weight_pounds is included in the model, its coefficient is 0.16171, meaning we would expect body fat percentage to increase by 0.16 percentage points with every additional pound of body weight. This flipping of signs–going from an increase to a decrease or vice versa–is not uncommon when you fit a model with highly correlated covariates and then fit a model with only one of them.

This behavior is related to another effect of using correlated variables. In order to demonstrate it, I need to recreate mod_02 but somehow de-correlate the variables. The way to do that is to regress Abdomen on Weight_pounds and obtain the residuals. Note that these residuals are uncorrelated with Weight_pounds.

mod_04 <- lm(formula = Abdomen ~ Weight_pounds,
             data = d)
cor(d$Weight_pounds,
    mod_04$residuals)
[1] 5.824739e-17

Now I’m going to regress Bodyfat on Weight_pounds and the residuals from the regression of Abdomen on Weight_pounds.

mod_05 <- lm(d$Bodyfat ~ d$Weight_pounds + mod_04$residuals)
summary(mod_05)

Call:
lm(formula = d$Bodyfat ~ d$Weight_pounds + mod_04$residuals)

Residuals:
    Min      1Q  Median      3Q     Max 
-9.5953 -2.9776 -0.0177  2.8974  9.1920 

Coefficients:
                  Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)    
(Intercept)      -10.09418    2.56164  -3.941 0.000153 ***
d$Weight_pounds    0.16171    0.01424  11.354  < 2e-16 ***
mod_04$residuals   1.04408    0.08918  11.707  < 2e-16 ***
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Residual standard error: 4.181 on 97 degrees of freedom
Multiple R-squared:  0.7328,    Adjusted R-squared:  0.7273 
F-statistic:   133 on 2 and 97 DF,  p-value: < 2.2e-16

Note the following:

  • The \(R^2\) for mod_05 is the same as for mod_02. The two models are explaining exactly the same amount of variance in the outcome.
  • The coefficient estimate for Weight_pounds is the same in mod_05 and in mod_03. Since Weight_pounds is uncorrelated with mod_04$residuals, controlling for mod_04$residuals doesn’t change the relationship between Weight_pounds and Bodyfat.
  • The variance of the coefficient estimate for Weight_pounds in mod_02 is much greater than the variance of the coefficient estimate for Weight_pounds in mod_05.
summary(mod_02)$coefficients[2,2]^2 / summary(mod_05)$coefficients[2,2]^2
[1] 4.741732

When explanatory variables in a model are correlated, the variances of their associated coefficients are inflated. In fact, the “variance inflation factor (VIF)” is the ratio of the variance of the coefficient estimate in a model to the variance of the same coefficient estimate in a model where the explanatory variables are uncorrelated. We can use the function vif from the package car to get the variance inflation factors:

vif(mod_02)
Weight_pounds       Abdomen 
     4.741732      4.741732

Different sources use different cutoffs for when a VIF is “too high”, but you shouldn’t blindly follow them. Sometimes you want correlated variables in a model. For example, interaction terms tend to be highly correlated with their component variables, but that doesn’t mean we should exclude them. You have to use your judgment, and consider the goals of your analysis.

It’s important to note that problems don’t just arise when two variables are correlated with each other. It could be the case that no two variables in a data set are highly correlated, but one of the variables is close to being a linear combination of the others, and that will also inflate the variances. I’ll simulate some data to illustrate this.

In this model, I regressed Y on eleven explanatory variables.

Yet, in the model, the variance of the coefficient associated with X11 is far higher than it would be if the explanatory variables were uncorrelated.

57.3 Problems

57.3.1 Problem 1

If the explanatory variables in a model are utterly uncorrelated, what should the VIFs be? (By the way, this might seem impossible, but you can perform principal components analysis on your explanatory variables to get uncorrelated variables that have the same information.)

TipSolution

If the explanatory variables are uncorrelated, their VIFs should be 1.

57.3.2 Problem 2

What will happen if I try to regress \(Y\) on \(X\) and \(X^2\), where \(X\) can only be 0 or 1?

TipSolution

R won’t provide a result for the \(X^2\) term. Note that if \(X = 0\) then \(X^2 = 0^2 = 0 = X\) and if \(X = 1\) then \(X^2 = 1^2 = 1 = X\), so \(X^2 = X\).
