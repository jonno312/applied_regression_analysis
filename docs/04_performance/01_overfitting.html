60  Overfitting

60.1 Learning Objective

Define, use, and interpret the term “overfitting”.

60.2 Lesson

60.2.1 Definition

As we’ve discussed, we usually want to make claims about a population but can only observe a sample. It’s important that the conclusions we draw from our sample are generalizable to the population. Even if our goal isn’t to generalize to some larger population, but is instead to make predictions, we want to make sure that we can accurately predict new data, not just the data in our sample. “Overfitting” is what happens when you create a model that describes your sample really well but does a poor job of generalizing to or predicting new data from the same population.

60.2.2 Conceptual Example

Suppose your roommate is from Portugal. You know he speaks Portuguese, he needs the shower at 7am, he likes to cook dinner, and on Saturdays he plays Frisbee with friends. So, you conclude that all Portuguese people speak Portuguese, shower at 7am, like to cook dinner, and play Frisbee with their friends on Saturdays. That’s an example of overfitting. You’ve done a great job of describing (and predicting) your roommate’s behavior, but there’s no reason to believe that the same descriptions and predictions will work for other people from the same country. (At least, not all of them; it is reasonable to predict that other people from Portugal speak Portuguese.)

60.2.4 Note

There are lots of things that limit our ability to generalize from a sample to the population, including a small sample size or a biased sampling scheme. “Overfitting” is generally not used to refer to the size of the sample or how it was collected. Instead, it refers to the relationship between the model and the sample. When talking about overfitting, we’re kind of taking the sample for granted. We can’t change the sample now that we have it, but we can change the model, and we want to make sure the model isn’t overfit to the data.

60.3 Problems

60.3.1 Problem 1

Propose a method for determining whether a model is overfit to a sample.

TipSolution

We could hold out some data from the sample (call this the “test set”) and fit our model to the rest (call this the “training set”). If the model is overfit to the training set, it will do a poor job of predicting the outcome in the test set.

60.3.2 Problem 2

True or false: A Type II error is the same thing as overfitting.

TipSolution

False. You can make a Type II error even when the model is perfect. By chance alone, there is always a non-zero chance of making a Type II error.
