92.1.1 Part (a)

Create a scatter plot of the residuals as a function of the fitted values. Include a horizontal line at zero.

plot(x = mod_02$fitted.values,
     y = mod_02$residuals,
     xlab = "Fitted Values",
     ylab = "Residuals",
     col = alpha(colour = "black",
                 alpha = 0.3))
abline(h = 0,
       col = "red")

What do you conclude?

It appears that for each fitted value, the residuals average out to zero. This is actually guaranteed to be the case, because we have a “saturated” model.

It also appears that there is relatively constant vertical spread of the residuals throughout the plot, which means the assumption of constant variance is met.

Note that there are only four possible fitted values. This is because there are two binary explanatory variables, yielding four possible combinations of values, i.e., \((0,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((1,0)\), and \((1,1)\).

Below, I’ve plotted a boxplot of the residuals for each possible combination of the explanatory variables. I haven’t changed the labeling of the axes so that you can see what R does by default. This plot also supports our assumptions of linearity and equal variance.

boxplot(formula = mod_02$residuals ~ mod_02$model$statins * mod_02$model$exercise)

92.1.2 Part (b)

Plot a boxplot, histogram, and q-q plot of the residuals.

boxplot(x = mod_02$residuals,
        ylab = "Residuals")
hist(x = mod_02$residuals,
     xlab = "Residuals",
     main = "")
qqnorm(mod_02$residuals)
qqline(mod_02$residuals)

What do you conclude?

Based on the histogram, the residuals are approximately bell-shaped. The boxplot reveals that the residuals are right-skewed, and the q-q plot seems to show that the residuals deviate from normal in their right tail. Still, the sample size is large, and my understanding is that the observations are independent (and identically distributed), so I’m not super concerned about this last assumption being met. Even if it isn’t, we can still interpret the p-values.

92.1.3 Part (c)

Interpret the intercept in real-world terms.

This is the expected LDL cholesterol for someone who is not on statins and who does not exercise.

92.1.4 Part (d)

Interpret the estimate for statinsyes in real-world terms. Interpret the results of the hypothesis test associated with this coefficient as well.

This is the expected difference in LDL cholesterol between someone who does use statins and someone who doesn’t, where neither of these people exercise. The fact that it’s negative means that we would expect someone who uses statins to have lower LDL cholesterol than someone who doesn’t (again, assuming neither of these people exercise).

How did I arrive at that interpretation? Define:

  • \(L_i\) is the LDL cholesterol for the \(i\)th individual.
  • \(S_i = 1\) if the \(i\)th person uses statins and \(S_i = 0\) otherwise.
  • \(E_i = 1\) if the \(i\)th person exercises and \(E_i = 0\) otherwise.

Our model is \[ L_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 S_i + \beta_2 E_i + \beta_3 S_i E_i + \epsilon_i \text{.} \] We’re trying to interpret \(\beta_1\), which is \[ \begin{align*} &E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 1,E_i = 0\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,E_i = 0\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (1) + \beta_2 (0) + \beta_3 (1) (0) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (0) + \beta_3 (0) (0)\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 - \left(\beta_0 \right) \\ &= \beta_1 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

The p-value for this coefficient is less than 0.05, so we reject the null hypothesis and conclude it’s not zero. (I didn’t tell you what alpha level to use, but 0.05 is a reasonable one.) Thus, we can conclude that there is a statistically significant difference in LDL cholesterol between people who use statins and people who don’t, assuming all of these people don’t exercise.

92.1.5 Part (e)

Interpret the estimate for exerciseyes in real-world terms. Interpret the results of the hypothesis test associated with this coefficient as well.

This is the expected difference in LDL cholesterol between someone who does exercises and someone who doesn’t, where neither of these people use statins. The fact that it’s negative means that we would expect someone who exercises to have lower LDL cholesterol than someone who doesn’t (again, assuming neither of these people use statins).

We’re trying to interpret \(\beta_2\), which is \[ \begin{align*} &E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,E_i = 1\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,E_i = 0\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (1) + \beta_3 (0) (1) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (0) + \beta_3 (0) (0)\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_2 - \left(\beta_0 \right) \\ &= \beta_2 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

The p-value for this coefficient is less than 0.05, so we reject the null hypothesis and conclude it’s not zero. Thus, we can conclude that there is a statistically significant difference in LDL cholesterol between people who exercise and people who don’t, assuming all of these people don’t use statins.

92.1.6 Part (f)

Interpret the estimate for statinsyes:exerciseyes in real-world terms. Interpret the results of the hypothesis test associated with this coefficient as well.

This is the expected difference in the expected change in LDL cholesterol associated with using statins between people who exercise and people who don’t. Because it has the opposite sign of statinsyes, we would expect the effect of statins among people who exercise to be closer to zero than the effect of statins among people who don’t exercise.

We’re trying to interpret \(\beta_3\), which is \[ \begin{align*} &E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 1,E_i = 1\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,E_i = 1\right) - \left(E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 1,E_i = 0\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,E_i = 0\right)\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (1) + \beta_2 (1) + \beta_3 (1) (1) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (1) + \beta_3 (0) (1)\right) - \beta_1 \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_2 \right) - \beta_1 \\ &= \beta_3 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

The p-value is high, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis, i.e., that this coefficient is zero. This tells us that the effect of statins is the same regardless of exercise status.

92.2.4 Part (d)

Interpret the estimate for statinsyes in real-world terms. Interpret the results of the hypothesis test associated with this coefficient as well.

This is the expected difference in LDL cholesterol between someone who does use statins and someone who doesn’t, where both of these people are white. The fact that it’s negative means that we would expect someone who uses statins to have lower LDL cholesterol than someone who doesn’t (again, assuming both of these people are white).

Define:

  • \(N_i = 1\) if the \(i\)th person is non-white and \(N_i = 0\) otherwise.

Our model is \[ L_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 S_i + \beta_2 N_i + \beta_3 S_i N_i + \epsilon_i \text{.} \] We’re trying to interpret \(\beta_1\), which is \[ \begin{align*} &E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 1,N_i = 0\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,N_i = 0\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (1) + \beta_2 (0) + \beta_3 (1) (0) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (0) + \beta_3 (0) (0)\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 - \left(\beta_0 \right) \\ &= \beta_1 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

The p-value for this coefficient is less than 0.05, so we reject the null hypothesis and conclude it’s not zero. Thus, we can conclude that there is a statistically significant difference in LDL cholesterol between people who use statins and people who don’t, among white people.

92.2.5 Part (e)

Interpret the estimate for nonwhiteyes in real-world terms. Interpret the results of the hypothesis test associated with this coefficient as well.

This is the expected difference in LDL cholesterol between someone who does is not white and someone who is, where neither of these people use statins. The fact that it’s positive means that we would expect someone who is not white to have higher LDL cholesterol than someone who is white (again, assuming neither of these people use statins).

We’re trying to interpret \(\beta_2\), which is \[ \begin{align*} &E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,N_i = 1\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,N_i = 0\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (1) + \beta_3 (0) (1) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (0) + \beta_3 (0) (0)\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_2 - \left(\beta_0 \right) \\ &= \beta_2 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

The p-value for this coefficient is greater than 0.05, so we cannot reject the null hypothesis. Thus, there is not a statistically significant difference in LDL cholesterol between people who are not white and people who are, assuming all of these people don’t use statins.

92.2.6 Part (f)

Interpret the estimate for statinsyes:nonwhiteyes in real-world terms. Interpret the results of the hypothesis test associated with this coefficient as well.

This is the expected difference in the expected change in LDL cholesterol associated with using statins between people who are not white and people who are. Because it has the opposite sign of , we would expect the effect of statins among nonwhite people to be closer to zero than the effect of statins among people who are white.

We’re trying to interpret \(\beta_3\), which is \[ \begin{align*} &E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 1,N_i = 1\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,N_i = 1\right) - \left(E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 1,N_i = 0\right) - E\left(L_i\middle|S_i = 0,N_i = 0\right)\right) \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (1) + \beta_2 (1) + \beta_3 (1) (1) - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 (0) + \beta_2 (1) + \beta_3 (0) (1)\right) - \beta_1 \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 - \left(\beta_0 + \beta_2 \right) - \beta_1 \\ &= \beta_3 \text{.} \end{align*} \]

The p-value is low, so we reject the null hypothesis, i.e., that this coefficient is zero. This tells us that the effect of statins depends on race.
